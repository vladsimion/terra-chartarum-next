A border transforms geographical difference into political division. A line drawn across a landscape can separate jurisdictions, divide communities, establish claims of sovereignty and redefine the relationship between people and the places they inhabit. Border asks how empires, states and political communities have used maps to define, defend and legitimise their claims. Surveys precede annexations; frontiers become national boundaries; geographical names displace older identities and histories. Yet borders are rarely as stable or self-evident as maps suggest. They come out of treaties, wars, administration and rival ideas of who belongs. The history of a region such as Dacia, mapped and remapped across nearly two millennia, shows the same ground given different boundaries by each power, and each generation of scholars, that drew it. Beneath every apparently definitive line lie earlier claims, overlapping allegiances and alternative geographies. This room reads borders not simply as divisions of territory, but as historical arguments about possession, authority and belonging.
