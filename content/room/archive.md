Maps are made for particular purposes and moments, but they often outlive both. Once their original function has passed, they become historical artefacts: material traces of vanished institutions, geographical assumptions and ways of understanding the world. Archive asks what happens to a map after it has done its job. It considers maps not only as representations to be evaluated for their accuracy, but as objects whose production, ownership, circulation and preservation form part of their historical meaning. Provenance, editions, copying, annotation and material condition all contribute to the evidence a map can provide. Equally significant are its absences: territories left blank, populations unrecorded, knowledge that never entered the surviving record. Such silences are not simply gaps to be filled; they can reveal the conditions under which geographical knowledge was produced and preserved. To read a map historically is to look beyond what it depicts and investigate the circumstances that brought it into existence, the journeys it subsequently made and the traces it has left behind.
