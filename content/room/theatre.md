Named after Abraham Ortelius's Theatrum Orbis Terrarum, this is the room where the map stops pretending to be the world and admits that it stages one. A map does not merely organise geographical information; it arranges knowledge for an audience, establishing relationships, hierarchies and interpretations through the act of display. The Theatre looks at how maps have been used to communicate ideas, persuade viewers and make complex systems intelligible. Here, cartography meets philosophy, visual culture and the history of ideas. A collection of maps can become an argument about the development of geographical thought; a historical GIS can reveal relationships that remain invisible in a conventional chronology; an essay can use the arrangement of its evidence to make the structure of an argument visible. Like any performance, such presentations depend upon selection, arrangement and perspective. They make certain relationships visible while leaving others outside the frame. The Theatre asks what happens when the world is organised for observation, and how the act of making knowledge visible shapes what an audience comes to understand.
