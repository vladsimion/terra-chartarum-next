A map is never simply a window onto the world. It is a constructed representation, shaped by the purposes, knowledge and assumptions of its maker. Projection, scale, symbol, orientation and survey are not neutral technical choices: each determines what can be seen, what must be distorted and what may disappear altogether. Map asks what each of those choices costs, and who decided it was worth paying. From the earliest attempts to depict the known world to the systems of modern cartography, every improvement in accuracy or usability has involved choices about what matters and what can be sacrificed. The sphere is flattened, the coastline generalised, the unknown filled with conjecture. These are not merely technical compromises; they reveal changing ideas about knowledge and authority. To study a map is therefore to study its construction: its methods, its makers, its intended audiences and the assumptions embedded in every line.
