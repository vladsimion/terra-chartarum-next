Terra Chartarum treats maps as primary historical sources. Every map has a maker, a market, a method of projection, things it leaves out, and things it wants its reader to believe. The work here is to identify those choices, understand what they meant, and say what the evidence lets us know.

The site brings together visual essays, a historical atlas built from shared map layers, a catalogue of maps and their makers, and a bibliography. Every historical claim is traceable to its sources, and where the evidence is thin or contested, the page says so.

Two ideas underpin the project.

The first, informed by J. B. Harley's work on the history of cartography, is that every map is an argument. It is a constructed object, shaped by a maker, a method, and a motive. Its persuasive power often lies in its appearance of neutrality: it presents itself as a description of the world, but it carries the interests and assumptions of the people who made it.

The second is a caution about precision. A finer survey or a truer projection always costs something somewhere else. And the more authoritative a map appears, the easier it can be to overlook what it excludes, obscures, or takes for granted.

## Who makes it

I'm Vlad Simion. I work as a data scientist and collect historical maps, particularly printed maps from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries. Terra Chartarum began as an attempt to read these objects more carefully: to understand where their lines came from, what purposes they served, and what can be established about the worlds they represent.

I'm not a professional historian, and the site makes no claim to be one. What I bring is the habits of my day job: recording the origins of claims, distinguishing between degrees of certainty, and making the reasoning behind an interpretation available for scrutiny. Sources are cited throughout, where a question needs a specialist I cite one, and corrections are welcome.

I was born in Romania and now live in England. That personal connection is one reason the project begins with Dacia and Transylvania: regions whose borders, names and maps have changed often enough to make them a good place to study how maps relate to the worlds they claim to describe.
