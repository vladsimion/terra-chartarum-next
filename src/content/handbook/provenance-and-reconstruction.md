<h2 id="provenance">Provenance</h2>

Every line and area in the Atlas carries a visual indication of how its geometry was established. Provenance is encoded through line patterns and fill textures, never through colour alone:

- **Documentary geometry** — solid line and solid fill. Geometry traced directly from a documentary source, such as a historical survey or a georeferenced map.
- **Reconstruction** — dashed line and diagonal hatching. A reasoned scholarly reconstruction where no surviving source provides the complete geometry.
- **Generalised (editorial)** — dash-dot line and cross-hatching. Geometry simplified or adapted for the Atlas from a more detailed source.
- **Present-day reference** — thin, dotted grey line with no fill. Modern geographical outlines provided for orientation.

The legend displays only the provenance categories represented in your active layers. Provenance describes how a feature's geometry was established; confidence in its identification is recorded separately and shown only in the inspector.

<h2 id="reconstruction">Reconstruction</h2>

A reconstruction is an interpretation grounded in evidence, not a direct observation. A Roman road known from scattered milestones and excavated sections, or a frontier inferred from the distribution of forts, must be traced between places where the evidence survives. The resulting line represents a scholarly argument about what may have connected those points, not a route or boundary documented in its entirety.

Dashed lines and hatching make this distinction visible. They ensure that a reconstructed geometry is never mistaken for a directly surveyed or documented one. Choose "Inspect" on the layer to see its source and methodology, and follow the links there to trace the reasoning behind the interpretation.

<h2 id="present-day-reference">Present-day reference</h2>

Modern country outlines and other contemporary geographical boundaries are shown as thin, dotted grey lines. They provide spatial orientation without competing visually with historical features. They are reference aids, not evidence for any historical period: a present-day national boundary, for example, tells us nothing in itself about the extent of a Roman province.

The outlines used in the base map are derived from Natural Earth and follow the same visual convention.
