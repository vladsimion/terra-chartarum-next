# Competing hypotheses register

Stage II Phase 0 (TCX-67), story TRANS-0-US5 (TCX-78). Research programme: "Research questions", "Standards of proof" and "Quantitative methods". Method: `FRAMEWORK.md` (epistemic rules, standards of proof, confidence scale).

**Status: draft for the author, 22 November 2026.** This register must be complete before any Phase 1 evidence is graded, so each finding can be scored against every hypothesis at once. That is the evidence register's `hypotheses` field: *supports*, *weakens* or *neutral*, per hypothesis.

**How to read it.**
- Each research question has a numbered set of hypotheses. Each hypothesis says what it predicts, which evidence it expects (by tier and dimension), and **what would weaken it**.
- Every set includes an *indeterminate* hypothesis: that the evidence cannot decide. It is a real outcome, not a failure, and it must also earn its place on evidence.
- The cautious middle position gets no default advantage (programme, "What the earlier critique taught us").
- **Presence, sovereignty and identity are kept apart.** Each hypothesis says which of the three it is about. Evidence for one is not evidence for another.
- The examples of expected evidence are **pointers for Phase 1, not findings**. None has been graded. Each must be sourced and graded before it counts for or against anything.
- Silence is weak evidence: an absence weakens a hypothesis only when weighed against the expected survival of records for that place and period (`FRAMEWORK.md`, epistemic rules).

The Stage III question (connections across the Carpathians) is not covered here. Its hypotheses belong to the Stage III grill (TCX-80).

## Q1. Population north of the Danube, 271 to c. 1200

*What does the combined archaeological, epigraphic, linguistic and documentary evidence allow us to say, and not say, about population continuity north of the Danube from 271 to about 1200?* This question is about **presence and language**. It is not about sovereignty, and not about identity in the modern national sense. "Continuity" is used in its linguistic-demographic sense: a Latin- or Romance-speaking population living in the area without a break.

### H1.1 Strong north-Danubian continuity
A Romance-speaking population stayed in former Dacia, Transylvania included, through the whole period, and Romanian formed there.
- **Predicts:**
  - provincial-Roman settlement and material culture continuing after 275 in excavated rural sites;
  - Latin Christian finds of the 4th to 6th centuries in context;
  - place- and river-names of Transylvania with a direct Latin to Romanian sound development;
  - Romanian communities with established land and institutions when the charters first name them.
- **Expects:** tier B (dated settlements, finds), tier C (toponymy, sound changes), tier A from the 13th century.
- **Weakened by:**
  - a settlement hiatus after c. 300 across the excavated former province, still there after correcting for excavation intensity (the settlement-density test);
  - the major Transylvanian river-names reaching Romanian through Slavic or Hungarian rather than directly from Latin;
  - Romanian–Albanian shared vocabulary and other Balkan contact features that need long contact south of the Danube;
  - first-attestation dates of Romanian place-names in Transylvania clustering late (the place-name layers test).

### H1.2 Strong south-Danubian formation and later migration
Romanian formed south of the Danube. Romanian speakers settled north of it, Transylvania included, from about the 12th to 13th centuries.
- **Predicts:**
  - Balkan contact features and Romanian–Albanian shared vocabulary;
  - Romanian place-names in Transylvania attested late and often through Slavic or Hungarian forms;
  - Vlachs named in Balkan sources well before Transylvanian ones;
  - written traces of settlement from the south (privileges, colonisation).
- **Expects:** tier C (lexicon, toponymy), tier D and A (Balkan and Hungarian sources), tier B (an absence of Romance-speaking material culture north of the Danube after the Roman period).
- **Weakened by:**
  - Romanised settlement continuing north of the Danube after 275, corrected for excavation intensity;
  - Transylvanian place-names of Latin origin with direct Romanian sound changes;
  - north-Danubian dialect features older than the proposed migration;
  - Romanians in the earliest Transylvanian charters already holding land and institutions, as an established population would.

### H1.3 A wider two-bank formation zone
Proto-Romanian formed over a broad area on both banks of the Danube, with movement in both directions. Transylvania lay in or beside that zone, but was not necessarily Romance-speaking everywhere and at all times.
- **Predicts:** mixed evidence that varies by region: some Romance presence in the north alongside southern contact features, and uneven settlement over time.
- **Expects:** the same tiers as H1.1 and H1.2, read region by region.
- **Weakened by:** evidence that clearly favours one bank everywhere. This could be unbroken Romance presence in every Transylvanian basin. It could also be no Romance presence north of the Danube until the 12th century.

### H1.4 Local persistence, regional discontinuity
Romance speakers survived in some parts of the north (for example the mountain margins, Banat or Oltenia) but not in the Transylvanian basin. The basin was later resettled from those neighbouring areas, not from the Balkans.
- **Predicts:** regional differences in the settlement record and in the earliest place-names; the basin later than the margins.
- **Weakened by:** a uniform record across basin and margins, whether of continuity or of hiatus.

### H1.0 Indeterminate
The evidence cannot tell H1.1 to H1.4 apart for this period. The best grade any of them can reach is *possible*.
- **Predicts:** each line of evidence fits at least two hypotheses once survival and excavation bias are allowed for.
- **Weakened by:** any line of evidence that, graded *probable* or better, fits one hypothesis and contradicts the others.

## Q2. Transylvania as a distinct political and administrative unit

*How did Transylvania become a distinct political and administrative unit within the Kingdom of Hungary, the Principality and the Habsburg Monarchy, and how did its borders shift?* This question is about **sovereignty and administration**.

### H2.1 A royal office first
Transylvania became distinct mainly through the voivodate, a royal military and administrative office of the 12th to 13th centuries. The estates' separate standing came after it.
- **Predicts:** the voivode's jurisdiction over the counties attested before any estate-level separateness; borders defined by royal acts.
- **Weakened by:** regional institutions attested before the office, or the voivode's jurisdiction stopping well short of the later territory.

### H2.2 An older regional structure taken over
Distinctness came from a pre-existing frontier region and its local structures, which the kingdom absorbed but did not dissolve.
- **Predicts:** early structures that pre-date or run alongside royal ones (border defences, local lordships); continuity in their limits.
- **Weakened by:** no attested structure before the royal organisation; boundaries drawn fresh by the kingdom.

### H2.3 Territorial distinctness only from 1541
Before the Principality, Transylvania was separate in administration but not as a territory. It became a distinct territorial unit only with the Principality, and was confirmed as one under the Habsburgs.
- **Predicts:** before 1541, documents treat it as a set of jurisdictions, not as a bounded land; maps and treaties bound it only later.
- **Weakened by:** a bounded "Transylvania" as one land in documents or maps well before 1541.

### H2.0 Indeterminate, or a different answer for each strand
Different strands (military, fiscal, legal, estates) became distinct at different times, so "when did it become distinct" has no single answer.
- **Weakened by:** the strands converging on one period.

## Q3. Estates, religions, economic roles and language groups

*How did legal estates, received religions and economic roles map onto language groups, and where did they not?* This question is about **presence and identity** (legal and confessional categories against language). Every use of "Romanian", "Hungarian" or "Saxon" names its sense: linguistic, legal, confessional or self-identified.

### H3.1 Close fit
The estates (nobility, Saxons, Székelys) and the received religions mapped closely onto language groups. Romanian speakers fell mostly outside both.
- **Predicts:** few Romanian speakers among the nobility or the privileged; serfdom concentrated in one language group; confession following language.
- **Weakened by:** substantial Romanian-speaking nobility by county or period; Hungarian-speaking serfs in large numbers; confession cutting across language.

### H3.2 Legal categories that cut across language
The estates were legal and social categories, not language groups. The Union of the Three Nations bound estates, not peoples, and the fit with language was loose.
- **Predicts:** mixed-language membership in each estate; status explained by law and land, not language.
- **Weakened by:** a close fit between estate and language in the urbaria and the noble registers.

### H3.3 A fit that tightened over time
The fit was loose in the Middle Ages and tightened with confessionalisation after the Reformation, the Church Union, and 18th-century state policy.
- **Predicts:** the language–estate and language–confession fit measured by period, rising over time.
- **Weakened by:** a fit that is constant, or that falls, over time.

### H3.0 Indeterminate
The surviving records (urbaria, registers, censuses) do not record language consistently enough to measure the fit before the 18th century.
- **Weakened by:** enough early records with a language marker to measure it.

## Q4. How cartographers named, bounded and subdivided Transylvania, c. 1500–1800

*How did European cartographers name, bound and subdivide Transylvania between about 1500 and 1800, and what does that reveal about contemporary political geography?* This question is about **representation of sovereignty**.

### H4.1 Maps follow the current borders
Maps record contemporary political and administrative borders with little lag.
- **Predicts:** border changes appearing on maps soon after the dated political change; little copying of outdated borders.
- **Weakened by:** a long lag between dated changes and their appearance (the cartographic naming and borders test).

### H4.2 Maps follow other maps
Maps pass on inherited models (for example Honterus and Ortelius). Their borders and names come from copying, not survey, and lag behind events.
- **Predicts:** clusters of maps with shared errors that a copying tree can trace; borders that match an older model rather than their own date.
- **Weakened by:** independent borders that track events even where a model existed.

### H4.3 Maps follow their patrons
Naming and bounding follow the political standpoint of the publisher or sponsor (for example Habsburg, Ottoman, Venetian or Dutch commercial).
- **Predicts:** systematic differences in borders and names by publisher or patron, beyond what copying explains.
- **Weakened by:** no difference by patron once the copying tree is controlled for.

### H4.0 Mixed
Each of H4.1 to H4.3 holds for different periods or publishers, and no single model explains the corpus.
- **Weakened by:** one model explaining most of the corpus.

## Q5. The national narratives and the 1986–89 controversy

*How were the Romanian and Hungarian national narratives built from the 18th century onwards, and how did the 1986 Erdély története and its Romanian rebuttals function in the 1980s?* This question is about **identity and historiography**. The symmetry rule applies to every hypothesis: each is tested on both traditions, and on the Saxon and Western ones.

### H5.1 Politics first
Both narratives were formed mainly in answer to political claims (rights, recognition, borders). Their content follows political need.
- **Predicts:** shifts in the narratives timed to political events; claims kept after the evidence for them was withdrawn; citation chains that reproduce interpretations without returning to sources.
- **Weakened by:** shifts that follow new evidence (excavations, editions) rather than political events.

### H5.2 Evidence first, politics in reception
The narratives were formed mainly by scholarly method and the evidence available. Politics shaped how they were received and used more than what they said.
- **Predicts:** content changes timed to new evidence; scholarly texts more hedged than their political uses.
- **Weakened by:** content changes that track political events, as in H5.1. This includes changes between editions with no new evidence: the four-witness collation of ET1986, ERT1989, HT1994 and HT2001 tests it directly.

### H5.3 The 1986–89 controversy as a proxy dispute
In the 1980s the controversy worked mainly as a dispute between two states about minority policy and international standing, carried on through historians.
- **Predicts:** the controversy timed to diplomatic events; state channels in its circulation (paid advertisements, official publishers); little engagement with the book's evidence.
- **Weakened by:** a controversy carried mainly in scholarly channels, engaging the evidence chapter by chapter.

### H5.0 Mixed
Different authors and episodes fit different hypotheses, so the period cannot be characterised as a whole.
- **Weakened by:** one hypothesis fitting most episodes on both sides.
